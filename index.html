Exploring the Vibrations of a Circular Membrane with a Low-Cost Optical Sensor

Objectives and goals

A circular membrane never rings at a single pitch

It vibrates in a family of (m, n) modes, each with its own frequency and its own pattern of moving and still regions. The shapes below are the theory: the analytic mode shapes of an ideal clamped circular membrane.

The fundamental

Strike a head anywhere and this is what dominates. The whole surface moves together, fastest at the middle, pinned at the rim. Everything else on this poster is measured against it.

A still line appears

Add one nodal diameter and half the head moves up while the other half moves down. The line between them never moves at all. Strike exactly there and you cannot excite this mode.

That last sentence turns out to be a test, and the Method part fires it as a control.

Two lines, four quarters

Each extra nodal diameter divides the head again. The frequency climbs with the pattern, in a ratio fixed by the zeros of the Bessel function and by nothing else.

Rings, not only lines

Nodes also come as circles. Once you know the fundamental you can predict where every one of these should sit, which is exactly what this campaign set out to check on a printed head.

Goal: build a low-cost, non-contact vibration pickup from a reflective infrared sensor pair, and characterize it by mapping the vibrations of a circular membrane's surface.

Background and setup

How do you watch a drumhead without touching it

The usual instrument is expensive

Mapping those patterns is normally the job of laser Doppler vibrometry: precision optics at precision prices. Shrinking that cost is an active research direction (Li et al. 2022).

Two questions shaped the experiment

How to excite the head, and how to watch it. We weighed a speaker, a magnetic coil and a mechanical strike, and settled on a small solenoid.

  • One sensor, one solenoid, and a fully 3D-printed drum: body, clamp ring, striker mount.
  • A two-axis stage automates the campaign, and four mountings cover the full head.

What we did not use, and why

A microphone hears the whole head and the room at once, so it gives a pitch with no map. A high-speed camera resolves the shapes (Worland 2010) at laser prices. Reflected light was the cheap way to keep spatial detail.

The pickup

Our pickup is the ST188, an infrared LED and phototransistor pair sold for line-following robots. It watches a broad radial patch of the head, not a single point like a laser: excellent at finding the frequency a membrane vibrates at, weaker at pinning a mode to one spot.

The first good recordings looked like noise. We were filtering that oscillation out of the trace when we recognized it as the membrane.
The head

The membrane will not hold still

Every campaign measures its own fundamental

We are using a 3D-printed 50 mm TPU membrane, clamped between two rings and stretched by tightening embedded screws. Its tension is neither continuous nor even, and nothing measures it, and it drops as the material degrades over the days.

This is 372 kept strikes, each one measured on its own. They land on top of each other.

Three days, three fundamentals

Freshly stretched the head rings at 323 Hz, a day later at 290 Hz (this campaign), and by the third day at 270 Hz.

Nothing was re-clamped in between. The head simply relaxes, which is why no frequency on this site is quoted without the campaign it came from.

So the ladder is anchored, not assumed

Only the ratios between modes are fixed by physics. Every predicted frequency here is that ratio times the fundamental this campaign measured for itself.

Data and results

Three peaks share the head

Take one narrow band of frequency at a time and ask how loud it is at every cell. Each map is scaled to its own maximum, so what matters here is the pattern and not the brightness.

289 Hz, the fundamental

Loud in the middle, dark at the rim. This is the mode the whole Bessel ladder is anchored on, and it behaves exactly as a fundamental should.

467 Hz, under study

The ladder anchored on 290 Hz predicts the (1,1) mode near 462 Hz. Two peaks turn up instead of one, and neither is a confirmed identification.

508 Hz, under study

A split pair is what an unevenly tensioned head looks like, and this head is unevenly tensioned by construction. That is a hypothesis, not a result. The placebo test has not confirmed either peak.

31 Hz is not the drum

Mains and light flicker reach the sensor as well. It is labelled electrical everywhere it appears, and it is never counted as a mode.

Conclusion and future work

What is settled and what is not

Settled

  • A reflective sensor costing about a dollar measures a printed drumhead's fundamental to about a hertz, over 372 strikes and four separate mountings.
  • Cells that more than one mounting visited agree well enough to glue four quadrants into one map. The number is in the Method part.
  • The head's tension drifts from day to day, so every campaign has to re-measure its own fundamental.

Open

  • 467 and 508 Hz. Theory anchored on this campaign predicts one mode near 462 Hz. Two peaks are there and the split is not explained.
  • Damping. Two independent ways of measuring the decay disagree on more than half the strikes, so no damping number is claimed here.
  • The lobe axis. The 23° axis this project uses was measured on the previous membrane and has never been re-measured on the TPU head.
Draft, for the operator. The conclusion and future work boxes on the printed poster are deliberately empty and are his to write. The idea he wrote in his own log, kept here verbatim so it is not lost: this measurement method could end up cheaply replacing laser Doppler vibrometry in cases where ultra precision is not needed but rather knowing the more general vibration of an object, such as its fundamental frequency, or detecting unwanted vibrations.

Method

How a peak earns the right to be called a mode

A drumhead spectrum has bumps in it. Most of them are not modes. What follows is every rule this project uses to tell the difference, each one drawn on the campaign's own data, and two of them with the threshold left in your hands.

scroll
Rule one

Measure on one branch, look at the other

Cleaning a signal enough to see it and cleaning it enough to measure it are different jobs. This project keeps them apart on purpose.

What the converter recorded

A 14-bit block at 8 kHz, 300 ms long, with a slice of quiet before the hit. Nothing has been done to it yet.

That quiet slice is not padding. Every signal-to-noise number on this site is the peak divided by the spread of those samples.

Despike

A sample that sits more than five robust deviations away from its neighbours is a converter glitch, and it is replaced by the local median. The comparison is against a median, so one bad sample cannot drag the test that judges it.

High pass at 5 Hz, forwards and backwards

The sensor also sees where the head is, not only how it moves, and that drifts. The filter runs in both directions so the phase it adds cancels: the onset of the strike stays exactly where it happened.

This is the measurement branch. Every frequency, amplitude and decay quoted anywhere on this site comes from here.

The gate that never touches a number

At the bench there is a fourth stage: a spectral gate that pushes anything close to the noise floor down by 30 dB. It makes a faint strike visible on screen.

It is also a liar about amplitude and about decay, so it is display only. Nothing is ever measured on the gated branch.

Rule two

A peak has to stand above its own neighbourhood

Not above zero, and not above some fixed number. Above the noise floor measured right where the peak is.

The spectrum of the whole campaign

Every kept strike, each normalised to its own peak first so a hard hit cannot outvote a soft one, then combined by median. A median is used rather than a mean so a single bad strike cannot invent a peak.

The floor, measured locally

The dotted line is the running median of the spectrum over a 120 Hz window. It follows the broad shape of the noise instead of assuming it is flat, which matters because it is not.

The bar is 10 dB, and you can move it

A candidate counts only if it stands 10 dB above that local floor. Drag the slider and watch peaks appear and vanish.

The point of showing you the knob is that the bar was fixed before the answer was known. Turn it up and the real peaks are the last ones standing.

Two regions are excluded outright

Below 100 Hz the hanging rig rings, sways and out-shouts the head, so nothing there is ever read as a membrane mode.

The mains harmonics are notched 3 Hz wide up to 360 Hz, and the harmonics notched are the ones actually measured in that session, not an assumed 50 or 60 and its multiples.

Rule three

One ridge is one mode, however many bumps it has

Applied here to the campaign median so you can watch it act. This project has two pickers: this one runs on every strike, and the one in the next rule chooses the campaign's published mode list. They do not always keep the same peaks, and where they differ the site shows both rather than picking a winner.

The naive answer is wrong

Rank every local maximum by height and the top of the list is not five modes. It is one ridge and four of its own flank bins.

This actually happened. A run once reported its five strongest modes as 323, 330, 315, 339 and 307 Hz. That is one peak, read five times.

Separation alone is not enough

Demanding 8 Hz between peaks removes the closest duplicates. The gap is not made wider than that, because this rig has resolved a real 8.8 Hz split before and a wider rule would have thrown it away.

Demand a valley between them

Two peaks are two modes only if the spectrum dips at least 3 dB below the weaker one somewhere between them. A bump on the shoulder of a bigger peak never clears that, however tall it looks on its own.

What this rule refuses to do

An earlier version of the picker reserved a slot for the strongest peak above 100 Hz, so that something in the membrane band always got reported. That biases the detector toward the answer we were hoping for, and it was removed. Peaks are ranked by magnitude, each has to clear the floor, and nothing is injected.

Rule four

A mode cares where you hit it

This is the test a spectrum alone cannot do, and the reason the whole campaign is a map instead of a single measurement.

Nodes are the fingerprint

A membrane mode has places that do not move. Strike a node and that mode stays quiet, however hard the hit. A tone coming from the room, or from the mains, has no such places.

The fundamental, mapped

Bright in the middle, dark at the rim, and the ratio between the bright and the dark parts of the map is a number: the 90th percentile over the 10th.

Drag the frequency and watch the pattern collapse as soon as you step off a real peak.

The electrical line, mapped

The 31 Hz line does not come from the drum, and its map is drawn with the same code and the same scale as the others.

It is not flat. It is bright over part of the head and its contrast number is high, which is the honest thing to report and not the tidy one. Contrast alone would have let this line through. What keeps it out is the 100 Hz floor, where the rig lives and no membrane mode does, plus the operator calling it electrical at the bench.

The bar, and the trap it closes

A candidate needs a contrast of 1.8 to survive, raised to 2.5 if the peak sits at the same frequency under every mounting. A drum mode moves when the head is re-tensioned. A room does not.

Rule five

Strike the one place the mode cannot be

Every rule so far asks whether a peak is there. This one asks whether it is still there when it has no right to be.

The problem with a search window

A hunt looks for a predicted mode within five percent of where the ladder says it should be. At 700 Hz that window is ±35 Hz wide, and anything inside it counts as a hit.

Repeating the same hunt three times does not help. It repeats the same mislabel three times.

So fire a control

Every mode has somewhere it cannot be excited. For any mode with a nodal diameter that place is the centre, which is a node of both members of a split pair. For the ringed modes it is the inner nodal circle.

The striker moves there. The sensor does not move at all, so the only thing that changed is where the head was hit.

Compare the band with itself

Each strike's level in the band is divided by that same strike's own noise floor before anything is compared, which cancels how hard the solenoid happened to hit.

The rule, and what it caught

A candidate is confirmed only if the median of the real strikes beats the loudest of the nulls by 6 dB. Median against maximum, deliberately, because the test should be hard to pass and easy to fail.

The 468 Hz candidate cleared its null by 2.3 dB and was marked SUSPECT. On another run the same candidate reached 5.8 dB, which is still a fail. That near miss is why the bar is a fixed number and not a judgement call.

Rule six

Where two methods disagree, say so

Two pickers, one strike

One finds peaks in the spectrum. The other fits the tail as a sum of decaying sinusoids and is not limited by the width of a frequency bin. Where both land on the same frequency, that frequency is trusted. Where only one does, it is a candidate.

Two decays, one ring-down

One integrates the energy backwards from the end. The other fits a straight line to the envelope in decibels. They should agree.

They do not agree often enough, and that is why the poster shows how long the head rings without putting a damping number on it.

A clipped strike is a lower bound

Hit the middle hard and the amplifier runs out of room. The peak is then whatever the rail is, which is not a measurement of the drum. Those strikes are kept, marked, and never averaged as if they were.

Does the map glue together

Four mountings, four coordinate frames, one membrane. The overlap strips are the only reason to believe the result is one map and not four unrelated ones.
